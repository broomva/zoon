# Pre-registration of the pilot

**Status: draft.** It becomes the registered version at the commit that also holds the
analysis code and the files listed under "Files that are part of this registration". From
that commit the repository is public. No call to a judge has been made and no held-out
participant has been analysed.

This file fixes what `docs/pilot/arms.md` left open. Where the two differ, this file governs.

## What is tested

Two hypotheses, each for one zoon.

- **H1-evaluative.** `zoon-evaluative` predicts held-out choices better than `prompted`,
  `laws-only` and its own `no-state` arm.
- **H1-strict.** The same for `zoon-strict`.

**The verdict of the pilot is H1-evaluative with the decision model as the judge.** It
passes if, for each of its three comparisons, both of these hold:

1. the mean difference in the primary measure is at least 0.002 nats per choice;
2. the lower end of the interval for that difference is above zero.

A difference of exactly 0.002 passes the first. A lower end of exactly zero fails the second.

The 0.002 is a floor of practical size, chosen here. With the spreads measured in
`docs/pilot/lean.md` it will rarely bind: an interval that clears zero already implies a
larger mean.

**The three comparisons are not equally informative.** On training participants, `lean.md`
measured that learning adds about the same amount on top of each lean it tried, including one
that carries nothing, and that the pilot has high power to detect it. So beating `no-state`
is expected on that evidence, though nothing guarantees it, and it says little about the
judge. The comparisons more likely to fail are those with `laws-only` and with `prompted`.

**What the rule shows.** A positive difference whose estimate is at or above the floor. It
does not show that the true difference is above 0.002. The three intervals are separate
intervals, not one joint interval.

H1-strict, and both hypotheses with the second model family, are computed the same way and
reported beside the verdict. They cannot change it.

**What a pass means.** That this pipeline predicted better than its three reduced versions
on these 13 dilemmas. It does not mean that the judge read the dilemmas afresh, and it is
not a pass of H1 as the design first wrote it, which is H1-strict.

A pass also does not show that the judge's rating adds anything beyond knowing which option
the suggested action is. `laws-only` lacks both the rating and that knowledge. The comparison
that separates them, `zoon-evaluative` minus `sign` with learning, is reported and decides
nothing.

The comparison with `prompted` is between this pipeline and one prompt, with the examples
and the fitting data described below. A loss by `prompted` is a loss by that prompt with that
much data, not by prompting in general.

## Data

Maier, Cheung and Lieder, https://osf.io/4up5z/ (CC-BY 4.0), fetched by `zoon.pilot.fetch`
and checked against `data-manifest.json`. All four experiments are pooled. The authors'
exclusion rules are applied as that module implements them.

**Split.** Participants are assigned to training or held-out by the committed rule in
`zoon.pilot.split`. The held-out set has 680 participants, and all of them are analysed.
A development error once printed the dilemma order and one answer of one held-out record
from experiment 2, as `docs/pilot/power.md` discloses. That participant is kept: no rule for
identifying the record was written down at the time, and choosing one now would be a choice
made late.

**Complete records only.** Every analysed participant has 13 valid trials. The coded arms,
and the arms that use the decision model, must give a finite probability for every trial in
both of its rotations. If either fails for anyone, the run stops and gives no verdict. A
failure in an arm that uses the second family removes that family's results and nothing
else.

**Rotations.** Dilemmas are held out two at a time, in the 13 neighbouring pairs of the fixed
order in `zoon.pilot.fetch.DILEMMAS`, so each dilemma is held out in two rotations.

## Primary measure

For one arm, one held-out participant and one trial: the log of the probability the arm gave
to the answer the participant chose. Each trial has two such logs, one from each rotation in
which its dilemma is held out, and they are averaged. A participant's score is the mean of
those averages over their 13 trials. A comparison is the mean, over held-out participants, of
the difference between two arms' scores.

In a rotation, an arm's parameters are those fitted on training participants in that
rotation. Nothing is fitted to a held-out participant.

**Interval.** `zoon.pilot.power.bootstrap_interval`, applied to the per-participant
differences ordered by experiment and then by participant index. It resamples participants
with replacement 10,000 times with seed 20261115 and takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
An interval from resampling the 13 dilemmas is reported beside it and decides nothing: the
difference is averaged over participants for each dilemma, and the same function is applied
to those 13 numbers.

**Probabilities.** Every probability is clamped to between 0.001 and 0.999 before its log is
taken.

## The coded arms

`logit P(cost-benefit option) = s(d) · (alpha + beta · m(d)) + tau · (v[cost-benefit] − v[rule])`

as defined in `docs/pilot/lean.md`. Values start at zero, and after each trial the chosen
option's value moves toward the participant's rating, divided by 100, by the learning rate.
A participant's values are updated on every trial they saw, including held-out dilemmas.

| Arm | `m(d)` | `tau` |
|---|---|---|
| `zoon-evaluative` | From the evaluative judge's rating | Fitted |
| `zoon-strict` | From the strict judge's predicted rating | Fitted |
| `no-state`, for each zoon | That zoon's `m(d)` | Zero |
| `laws-only` | None: the `pooled` baseline of `lean.md` | Fitted |

In each rotation, every arm's parameters are fitted by maximum likelihood on training
participants and the 11 dilemmas not held out, with the temperature prior, bounds, starting
points and optimiser already in `zoon.pilot.models` and `zoon.pilot.lean`. The two feedback
conditions share parameters.

**Standardising.** In each rotation, the mean and the population standard deviation of a
judge's raw ratings are taken over the 11 dilemmas not held out, and all 13 ratings are
centred and scaled by them. The sign `s(d)` is applied afterwards. If that standard deviation
is below 1e-9, `m(d)` is zero for every dilemma in that rotation. The reference rows' ratings
are standardised in the same way.

## The judge

The judge is given the `rewrite` of one dilemma from `pilot/rewrites/`, with its final
question removed, and nothing else. The text given is the first part returned by
`zoon.pilot.rewrites._question`, with trailing white space removed. The set of rewrites is
fixed by the digest in `pilot/rewrites.sha256`.

### The evaluative question

The wording of the original paper's US study:

> How appropriate is it to {suggested_action}?

with seven answers, 1 to 7, where 1 is "completely inappropriate" and 7 is "completely
appropriate", and 2 to 6 carry no label. The paper's method section gives these labels for
the US study; a footnote to one of its tables gives "not at all appropriate" for 1. The
method section is followed.

The raw rating is the sum over the seven answers of the answer times its probability.

### The strict questions

One call per question, in these words:

1. "Does the suggested action involve killing a person?"
2. "Are the people who would be harmed by the suggested action among the people who would
   benefit from it?"
3. "Would the person deciding benefit personally from the suggested action?"
4. "Would it be reasonable to describe this dilemma the other way round, asking about the
   opposite action?"
5. "Which of these contexts does the dilemma belong to?", with the nine contexts of the
   original paper as the answers, in the order they first appear in
   `src/zoon/pilot/korner_deutsch_2022.csv`.

Each of the first four is followed by the sentence "The suggested action is:
{suggested_action}." and takes the probability of "Yes". The fifth takes the probability of
each context. These are the features of the `coded` lean in `lean.md`, with the judge's
probabilities in place of published codes. Whether the action is the cost-benefit option is
taken from the task and not asked. The map from features to rating is fitted on the judge's
answers for the 25 outside rewrites by the procedure in `zoon.pilot.lean`, unchanged.

That procedure may choose no map, which makes `m(d)` zero for every dilemma. With the
published codes it did. If it does so here, the result is reported as "these descriptive
questions carried no usable lean", with the penalty chosen. Beside it are given, for the best
finite penalty, the leave-one-out error and correlation on the 25 and the ratings it predicts
for the 13. No further arm is fitted with it. It is not reported as a finding about descriptive judges in general. One
of the nine contexts is coded for four task dilemmas and for none of the 25, so with the
published codes its weight could not be learned.

### How each family is asked, and where the probabilities come from

**Decision model.** One question per call, with the declared answers. The rewrite is the
state. It returns one probability per declared answer, rounded to two decimal places. They
are divided by their sum. If the sum is not within 0.05 of one, the reply is treated as a
fault of the decision model. Each declared answer is described to
the model by the answer itself, except the two ends of the evaluative scale, which are
described as "1 (completely inappropriate)" and "7 (completely appropriate)".

**Second family.** The request is the rewrite, a blank line, the question, and one closing
line:

- for the evaluative question: "Answer with one digit from 1 (completely inappropriate) to 7
  (completely appropriate).";
- for a yes or no question: "Answer with one word: Yes or No.";
- for the context question: the nine contexts lettered A to I, then "Answer with one letter.".

Every such request also carries one system instruction: "Reply with the answer only: a
single digit, word or letter, with nothing before or after it." It was added before
registration, after trials on two made-up dilemmas that are not part of the pilot showed
that without it the model often began its reply with a sentence. The `prompted` arm's request
to the second family carries it too. The recognition request does not.

The probability of an answer is the probability of its first token, summed over the variants
of that token with and without a leading space, among the 20 likeliest tokens returned. An
answer that is not among them gets zero. The probabilities of the declared answers are then
rescaled to sum to one. The share of probability that fell on declared answers before
rescaling is recorded for every call and summarised in the report: the number of calls, the
lowest, the mean and the highest share, and the number below one half. If it is below one
half for any call to that family, as a judge or in the `prompted` arm, the family is reported
as unusable and its results are not given. A reply from the second family that cannot be
read, for instance one that the service blocked or that carries no log-probabilities, counts
as a share of zero.

## Checking the judge, and the stop rule

Before any other call, each family's evaluative judge rates the 25 outside rewrites. The
check is the Pearson correlation, over those 25, between its raw rating and the mean rating
of the original dilemma in the paper's US sample, computed from the paper's data files by
`zoon.pilot.outside`.

**Stop rule.** The pilot goes on only if that correlation, for the decision model, is a
finite number and at least 0.50. Otherwise it stops. No held-out participant is read, and the
outcome is reported as "the judge could not be calibrated". It is not a result about either
hypothesis. If the second family fails the same test, its arms are not run and that is
reported.

The 0.50 is a choice made here, as a precaution. With 25 dilemmas, a correlation of 0.50
puts the one-sided 95% lower bound above zero. A judge only weakly related to human ratings
may do harm: in `lean.md` the one weakly related lean that was tried cost more than it
gained. Passing the threshold does not guarantee that the rating carries over to the 13 task
dilemmas.

The strict judge's agreement with the original paper's codes is reported and stops nothing.
It is the mean absolute difference between the judge's probability and the code, for each
feature asked, over the 25 and over the 13.

## The `prompted` arm

### What the model is sent

```
A person is taking part in a study. They read moral dilemmas one at a time. For each one
they answer Yes or No to the question at the end. They are then told what happened as a
result, and they rate how good or bad the consequences were, from -100 (very bad) to 100
(very good).

Here are records from other people in the same study.

{examples}

Here is the record of the person you are asked about.

{record}
```

A **trial** is rendered as:

```
Dilemma:
{the dilemma's text as participants read it, including its question}
Answer: {Yes or No}
What happened: {the outcome text shown after that answer}
Rating: {the participant's rating, an integer from -100 to 100}
```

An **example** is the line "Person A:" for the first example and "Person B:" for the second,
then that training participant's first trials in order, each rendered as a trial, then their
next trial rendered with its "Dilemma:" lines and its "Answer:" line, which holds the answer
they gave. Its outcome and rating are left out.

The **record** is the person's own earlier trials in order, all of them, each rendered as a
trial, or the line "This person has not answered any dilemma yet.", followed by:

```
Current dilemma:
{the dilemma's text}
```

Parts are separated by one blank line. The question is "Which answer does this person give
to the current dilemma?", with the answers Yes and No. The decision model receives the text
above as state and the question with its two declared answers. The second family receives the
text, a blank line, the question, and "Answer with one word: Yes or No.".

The dilemma texts and outcome texts are those of the task's published materials, committed
in `pilot/task-texts.json` with attribution.

**The committed renderer fixes every character.** `zoon.pilot.prompts` builds every request,
and `prereg/requests/` holds what it produces for each kind of request. This section
describes them. For the exact characters of a request, the committed renderer and its
rendered requests govern.

### Examples

There are two examples in every prompt, fixed for each rotation in `prereg/cohorts.json`.
Every prompt in a rotation uses the same two.

An example never contains a held-out dilemma and never has a gap. The trial it ends on comes
before the first trial at which that participant met either dilemma held out in the rotation,
and is not their first trial. So an example is an unbroken run from the participant's first
trial, with at least one complete trial before the one it ends on.

The person's own record does include their earlier trials on held-out dilemmas, as the coded
arms' histories do.

**What this gives the arm, and what it does not.** Examples are short by construction,
because they stop before a held-out dilemma; their lengths are in `prereg/cohorts.json`.
Beyond its two examples, the arm has two numbers fitted to 300 training participants. The
coded arms are fitted to all 1,648. The arms do not have equal data, and the comparison is
not claimed to be one of equal data.

The interval treats the examples and all fitted numbers as fixed. It does not include what
another draw of examples would change.

### From the model's answer to a probability

With `p` the model's probability of "Yes":

1. `p1 = clip(p, 0.001, 0.999)`
2. `p2 = p1` where the suggested action is the cost-benefit option, and `1 − p1` otherwise
3. `q = clip(sigmoid(a · logit(p2) + c), 0.001, 0.999)`

`q` is the arm's probability of the cost-benefit option. In each rotation `a` and `c`
maximise the sum of the logs of the probabilities `q` gives to the answers chosen, over the
calibration subsample's trials on the 11 dilemmas not held out. The fit is by L-BFGS-B from
`a = 1`, `c = 0`, with `a` and `c` each between −10 and 10. `a` may be negative, as the
coded arms' `beta` may. If the optimiser reports failure, `a = 1` and `c = 0` are used and
the failure is reported.

The calibration subsample is 300 training participants, fixed in `prereg/cohorts.json`.

This arm reads the task's own texts, not the rewrites. It may have seen the dataset or the
paper in training, and no adjustment is made for that.

## Files that are part of this registration

They are committed in the same change as the analysis code, and a test fails if any of them
differs from what the code regenerates.

| File | Holds |
|---|---|
| `pilot/rewrites/` and `pilot/rewrites.sha256` | The texts a judge is shown |
| `pilot/task-texts.json` | Each dilemma's text as participants read it, and the outcome text for each answer and outcome |
| `prereg/cohorts.json` | The calibration subsample, and for each rotation the two example participants and the trials their examples end on, by participant index |
| `prereg/requests/` | One rendered request of each kind for each family, including a record with no earlier trials |

`prereg/cohorts.json` is produced by `zoon.pilot.cohorts`, in exactly this way:

```
rng   = numpy.random.Generator(numpy.random.PCG64(20261115))
ids   = training participants, sorted by experiment and then participant index
order = rng.permutation(len(ids))
calibration = [ids[i] for i in order[:300]]
rest        = [ids[i] for i in order[300:]]
for each rotation, in the order of the 13 neighbouring pairs:
    first    = for each participant in rest, the position (from 1) of their first
               trial on either dilemma held out in this rotation
    eligible = participants in rest with first >= 3, in the order of rest
    a, b     = rng.choice(len(eligible), size=2, replace=False)
    for the participant at a, then the participant at b:
        last = int(rng.integers(2, first))   # from 2 up to first - 1
```

`last` is the position of the trial the example ends on. The participant at `a` is Person A
and the one at `b` is Person B. The same participant may be drawn in more than one rotation.

## Models

| Family | Model | Settings |
|---|---|---|
| Decision model, which decides | `jev-1.14.0` | One question per call, with declared answers |
| Second family, reported | `gemini-2.5-flash-lite` | Temperature 0; thinking off; one output token; log-probabilities of the 20 likeliest tokens; the service's content filters off |

The version each response reports is recorded. The decision model must report exactly the
version above. The second family's version is the one its first response reports, which must
begin with the name above. If the decision model is withdrawn or reports another version
before the run ends, the run stops and gives no verdict. If the second family does, its
results are not given.

## Spending

Live calls stop at 150 US dollars. A call is not sent if what has been spent, plus the
estimate below for that call and for the calls still in flight, would pass the cap. If the
cap is reached before the run ends, the run stops and gives no verdict.

Spending is counted from the tokens each response reports, at the prices in
`zoon.pilot.prices`, which records the page and the date each price was read from.

The number of examples is fixed at two before registration, by a bound that needs no
held-out data and no call to a model. `uv run python -m zoon.pilot.report budget` prints it.

- A request's input tokens are taken as its characters divided by three, rounded up. The
  decision model is charged 400 more per call. A request's characters are those of the form
  committed in `prereg/requests/`: the text for the second family, and the state, question
  and answers together for the decision model.
- Judge, recognition and calibration requests are the real ones.
- Held-out requests are bounded: 680 participants, 13 trials and two rotations, each request
  taken as the rotation's two examples, the 12 longest renderings of a trial over every
  dilemma, answer, outcome and rating, and the longest dilemma.

This is an estimate of tokens, not a count, and it is meant to be high. What limits spending
is the cap. Registration goes ahead only if the bound is at most 120 US dollars. If it is
not, this file is revised and reviewed again before registration; examples are never reduced
after it.

## Order of work

1. This file, the files above and the analysis code are merged. The repository becomes
   public.
2. Each family's evaluative judge rates the 25 outside rewrites. The stop rule is applied.
3. Each family's judges answer for the 13 task rewrites and the strict questions for all 38.
   Each family's evaluative judge rates the reversed rewrites. The recognition check is run.
4. The `prompted` arm is called for the calibration subsample.
5. The held-out participants are loaded. The `prompted` arm is called for them.
6. One command produces the report, which is committed with a digest of every transcript.

Held-out participants are not loaded, counted beyond their number, or rendered into a
request before step 5.

**Repairs.** After step 2 begins, the code may change only to fix a fault that stops it
running, and only if the fix leaves every request, every inclusion rule and every numerical
definition in this file unchanged. Any other change ends the confirmatory run: what follows
is reported as exploratory, and the hypotheses are not decided by it.

A fault of the decision model that is not a wrong version, such as a reply that cannot be
read after the retries, stops the step. The step can be run again and sends only the requests
that have no recorded answer.

The run checks repairs as far as code can. Each step records digests of this file, of the files
listed above and of the code. A step refuses to go on if this file or a listed file has
changed. If only the code has changed, it goes on only when told that the change is a repair
and why. The report lists every repair: the reason given, the commit, and which files
changed. Whether a repair kept every definition unchanged is for a reader to judge from that
commit; the run cannot certify it.

## Reported beside the verdict

- H1-strict, and both hypotheses with the second family.
- The reference rows of `lean.md` with no model: `sign`, `coded`, `norm` and `known share`,
  each with learning as well. `zoon-evaluative` minus `sign` with learning is given, since it
  isolates what the judge's rating adds beyond knowing which option the action is. As in
  `lean.md`, `known share` is fitted to training answers on all 13 dilemmas; it is a ceiling
  and not an arm.
- Each comparison by experiment, and on trials 2 to 13 only, each with its mean and its
  interval over participants.
- The interval from resampling dilemmas.
- The size of the reading: each zoon's gain over `laws-only`, divided by the gain of `norm`
  with learning over `laws-only`, and by that of `known share` with learning.
- The recall checks of `arms.md`. Recognition is specified below. Reversal is the judge's
  rating of a reversed rewrite minus its rating of the first rewrite, in scale points, for
  every reversed rewrite. Agreement with `norm` is the Pearson correlation, over the 13 task
  dilemmas, between the judge's raw rating and the US sample's mean rating, reflected as in
  `lean.md` where the task asks about the opposite action.
- Each arm's number of fitted parameters.
- Calls, tokens and cost by family.

### The recognition check

It is run for the 13 task rewrites with the second family only, because the decision model
cannot write text. So it says nothing direct about the model that decides. It needs text and
not probabilities, so it is run whatever the second family's own gates decided.

The model is sent a rewrite in full, a blank line, and the line "This describes a real case with the
details removed. Name the case, its place and its year, or answer unknown.", at temperature 0
with up to 40 output tokens.

A reply is **flagged** if it does not contain the word "unknown" and it contains a key of
that dilemma. The keys of a dilemma are every entry of its rewrite's `removed` list and the
name of the dilemma it was adapted from, as given by `zoon.pilot.outside.originals`. Both the
reply and the key are lowered and split into runs of letters and digits; a key is contained
if its runs appear in the reply in order, next to each other.

**A flag is a string match and nothing more.** It means the reply stated a detail that the
rewrite had removed, or the dilemma's published name. That can be a guess: "European" and
"December" are keys. A reply with no flag can come from a model that knows the case. The
flags are not evidence that a rating was recalled, or that it was not. Every reply is
published with its flag.

Every comparison is repeated with the flagged dilemmas dropped from the scores of all arms,
`prompted` included. Nothing is refitted and no model is called again, so the flagged
dilemmas stay in every history and every fit: this removes them as targets, not as
influences. If all 13 are flagged, the repeated comparisons are reported as not estimable.
None of this can change the verdict.
## What is not tested

Memory that decays, needs, and decision timing. The spread of behaviour across zoons. Any
dilemma other than these 13.

## Deviations

None.
