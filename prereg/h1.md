# Pre-registration of the pilot

**Status: draft.** It becomes the registered version at the commit that also holds the
analysis code and the files listed under "Files that are part of this registration". From
that commit the repository is public. No call to a judge has been made and no held-out
participant has been analysed.

This file fixes what `docs/pilot/arms.md` left open. Where the two differ, this file governs.

## What is tested

Two hypotheses, each for one zoon.

- **H1-evaluative.** `zoon-evaluative` predicts held-out choices better than `prompted`,
  `laws-only` and its own `no-state` arm.
- **H1-strict.** The same for `zoon-strict`.

**The verdict of the pilot is H1-evaluative with the decision model as the judge.** It
passes if, for each of its three comparisons, both of these hold:

1. the mean difference in the primary measure is at least 0.002 nats per choice;
2. the lower end of the interval for that difference is above zero.

A difference of exactly 0.002 passes the first. A lower end of exactly zero fails the second.

The 0.002 is a floor of practical size, chosen here. With the spreads measured in
`docs/pilot/lean.md` it will rarely bind: an interval that clears zero already implies a
larger mean.

**The three comparisons are not equally informative.** `lean.md` shows that learning adds
about the same amount on top of any lean, including one that carries nothing. So beating
`no-state` is close to assured by the learning rule and says little about the judge. The
comparisons that can fail are those with `laws-only` and with `prompted`.

H1-strict, and both hypotheses with the second model family, are computed the same way and
reported beside the verdict. They cannot change it.

**What a pass means.** That this pipeline predicted better than its three reduced versions
on these 13 dilemmas. It does not mean that the judge read the dilemmas afresh, and it is
not a pass of H1 as the design first wrote it, which is H1-strict.

## Data

Maier, Cheung and Lieder, https://osf.io/4up5z/ (CC-BY 4.0), fetched by `zoon.pilot.fetch`
and checked against `data-manifest.json`. All four experiments are pooled. The authors'
exclusion rules are applied as that module implements them.

**Split.** Participants are assigned to training or held-out by the committed rule in
`zoon.pilot.split`. The held-out set has 680 participants, and all of them are analysed.
A development error once printed the dilemma order and one answer of one held-out record
from experiment 2, as `docs/pilot/power.md` discloses. That participant is kept: no rule for
identifying the record was written down at the time, and choosing one now would be a choice
made late.

**Complete records only.** Every analysed participant has 13 valid trials, and every arm
must give a finite probability for every trial in both of its rotations. If either fails for
anyone, the run stops and gives no verdict.

**Rotations.** Dilemmas are held out two at a time, in the 13 neighbouring pairs of the fixed
order in `zoon.pilot.fetch.DILEMMAS`, so each dilemma is held out in two rotations.

## Primary measure

For one arm, one held-out participant and one trial: the log of the probability the arm gave
to the answer the participant chose. Each trial has two such logs, one from each rotation in
which its dilemma is held out, and they are averaged. A participant's score is the mean of
those averages over their 13 trials. A comparison is the mean, over held-out participants, of
the difference between two arms' scores.

In a rotation, an arm's parameters are those fitted on training participants in that
rotation. Nothing is fitted to a held-out participant.

**Interval.** `zoon.pilot.power.bootstrap_interval`, applied to the per-participant
differences ordered by experiment and then by participant index. It resamples participants
with replacement 10,000 times with seed 20261115 and takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
An interval from resampling the 13 dilemmas is reported beside it and decides nothing.

**Probabilities.** Every probability is clamped to between 0.001 and 0.999 before its log is
taken.

## The coded arms

`logit P(cost-benefit option) = s(d) · (alpha + beta · m(d)) + tau · (v[cost-benefit] − v[rule])`

as defined in `docs/pilot/lean.md`. Values start at zero, and after each trial the chosen
option's value moves toward the participant's rating, divided by 100, by the learning rate.
A participant's values are updated on every trial they saw, including held-out dilemmas.

| Arm | `m(d)` | `tau` |
|---|---|---|
| `zoon-evaluative` | From the evaluative judge's rating | Fitted |
| `zoon-strict` | From the strict judge's predicted rating | Fitted |
| `no-state`, for each zoon | That zoon's `m(d)` | Zero |
| `laws-only` | None: the `pooled` baseline of `lean.md` | Fitted |

In each rotation, every arm's parameters are fitted by maximum likelihood on training
participants and the 11 dilemmas not held out, with the temperature prior, bounds, starting
points and optimiser already in `zoon.pilot.models` and `zoon.pilot.lean`. The two feedback
conditions share parameters.

**Standardising.** In each rotation, a judge's raw ratings of the 13 dilemmas are centred and
scaled by the mean and standard deviation of the 11 dilemmas not held out. The sign `s(d)` is
applied afterwards. If that standard deviation is below 1e-9, `m(d)` is zero for every
dilemma in that rotation.

## The judge

The judge is given the `rewrite` of one dilemma from `pilot/rewrites/`, with its final
question removed, and nothing else. The final question is the last sentence, which begins
"Do you". The set of rewrites is fixed by the digest in `pilot/rewrites.sha256`.

### The evaluative question

The wording of the original paper's US study:

> How appropriate is it to {suggested_action}?

with seven answers, 1 to 7, where 1 is "completely inappropriate" and 7 is "completely
appropriate", and 2 to 6 carry no label. The paper's method section gives these labels for
the US study; a footnote to one of its tables gives "not at all appropriate" for 1. The
method section is followed.

The raw rating is the sum over the seven answers of the answer times its probability.

### The strict questions

One call per question, in these words:

1. "Does the suggested action involve killing a person?"
2. "Are the people who would be harmed by the suggested action among the people who would
   benefit from it?"
3. "Would the person deciding benefit personally from the suggested action?"
4. "Would it be reasonable to describe this dilemma the other way round, asking about the
   opposite action?"
5. "Which of these contexts does the dilemma belong to?", with the nine contexts of the
   original paper as the answers, in the order they first appear in
   `src/zoon/pilot/korner_deutsch_2022.csv`.

Each of the first four is followed by the sentence "The suggested action is:
{suggested_action}." and takes the probability of "Yes". The fifth takes the probability of
each context. These are the features of the `coded` lean in `lean.md`, with the judge's
probabilities in place of published codes. Whether the action is the cost-benefit option is
taken from the task and not asked. The map from features to rating is fitted on the judge's
answers for the 25 outside rewrites by the procedure in `zoon.pilot.lean`, unchanged.

That procedure may choose no map, which makes `m(d)` zero for every dilemma. With the
published codes it did. If it does so here, the result is reported as "these descriptive
questions carried no usable lean", with the penalty chosen and the result at the best finite
penalty beside it. It is not reported as a finding about descriptive judges in general. One
of the nine contexts occurs in four task dilemmas and in none of the 25, so its weight cannot
be learned.

### How each family is asked, and where the probabilities come from

**Decision model.** One question per call, with the declared answers. The rewrite is the
state. It returns one probability per declared answer.

**Second family.** The request is the rewrite, a blank line, the question, and one closing
line:

- for the evaluative question: "Answer with one digit from 1 (completely inappropriate) to 7
  (completely appropriate).";
- for a yes or no question: "Answer with one word: Yes or No.";
- for the context question: the nine contexts lettered A to I, then "Answer with one letter.".

The probability of an answer is the probability of its first token, summed over the variants
of that token with and without a leading space, among the 20 likeliest tokens returned. An
answer that is not among them gets zero. The probabilities of the declared answers are then
rescaled to sum to one. The share of probability that fell on declared answers before
rescaling is reported for every call. If it is below one half for any call, that family's
judge is reported as unusable and its arms are not run.

## Checking the judge, and the stop rule

Before any other call, each family's evaluative judge rates the 25 outside rewrites. The
check is the Pearson correlation, over those 25, between its raw rating and the mean rating
of the original dilemma in the paper's US sample.

**Stop rule.** If that correlation is below 0.50 for the decision model, the pilot stops.
No held-out participant is read, and the outcome is reported as "the judge could not be
calibrated". It is not a result about either hypothesis. If the second family is below 0.50,
its arms are not run and that is reported.

The 0.50 is a choice made here. With 25 dilemmas, a correlation of 0.50 puts the one-sided
95% lower bound above zero, and a judge that is merely positively related to human ratings
would be expected to do harm: in `lean.md` a weakly related lean cost more than it gained.

The strict judge's agreement with the published codes is reported and stops nothing.

## The `prompted` arm

### What the model is sent

```
A person is taking part in a study. They read moral dilemmas one at a time. For each one
they answer Yes or No to the question at the end. They are then told what happened as a
result, and they rate how good or bad the consequences were, from -100 (very bad) to 100
(very good).

Here are records from other people in the same study.

{examples}

Here is the record of the person you are asked about.

{record}
```

A **trial** is rendered as:

```
Dilemma:
{the dilemma's text as participants read it, including its question}
Answer: {Yes or No}
What happened: {the outcome text shown after that answer}
Rating: {the participant's rating, an integer from -100 to 100}
```

An **example** is the line "Person A:" or "Person B:", then that training participant's
trials in order up to but not including a chosen trial, with every trial on a dilemma held
out in the rotation left out, then the chosen trial with only its first two lines and
"Answer:".

The **record** is the person's own earlier trials in order, all of them, or the line "This
person has not answered any dilemma yet.", followed by:

```
Current dilemma:
{the dilemma's text}
```

The question is "Which answer does this person give to the current dilemma?", with the
answers Yes and No. The decision model receives the text above as state and the question
with its two declared answers. The second family receives the text, a blank line, the
question, and "Answer with one word: Yes or No.".

The outcome texts are those of the task's published materials, committed in
`pilot/outcome-texts.json` with attribution.

### Examples

There are two examples in every prompt. For each rotation they are fixed in
`prereg/cohorts.json`: two training participants outside the calibration subsample, and for
each the trial the example ends on, which is on a dilemma not held out in that rotation.
Every prompt in a rotation uses the same two examples. No example shows any trial on a
held-out dilemma. The person's own record does include their earlier held-out trials, as the
coded arms' histories do.

### From the model's answer to a probability

With `p` the model's probability of "Yes":

1. `p1 = clip(p, 0.001, 0.999)`
2. `p2 = p1` where the suggested action is the cost-benefit option, and `1 − p1` otherwise
3. `q = clip(sigmoid(a · logit(p2) + c), 0.001, 0.999)`

`q` is the arm's probability of the cost-benefit option. In each rotation `a` and `c`
maximise the sum of the logs of the probabilities `q` gives to the answers chosen, over the
calibration subsample's trials on the 11 dilemmas not held out. The fit is by L-BFGS-B from
`a = 1`, `c = 0`, with `a` between 0 and 10 and `c` between −10 and 10. If the optimiser
reports failure, `a = 1` and `c = 0` are used and the failure is reported.

The calibration subsample is 300 training participants, fixed in `prereg/cohorts.json`.

This arm reads the task's own texts, not the rewrites. It may have seen the dataset or the
paper in training, and no adjustment is made for that.

## Files that are part of this registration

They are committed in the same change as the analysis code, and a test fails if any of them
differs from what the code regenerates.

| File | Holds |
|---|---|
| `pilot/rewrites/` and `pilot/rewrites.sha256` | The texts a judge is shown |
| `pilot/outcome-texts.json` | The outcome text for each dilemma, answer and outcome |
| `prereg/cohorts.json` | The calibration subsample, and for each rotation the two example participants and the trials their examples end on, by participant index |
| `prereg/requests/` | One rendered request of each kind for each family, including a record with no earlier trials |

`prereg/cohorts.json` is produced by `zoon.pilot.cohorts` from training participants ordered
by experiment and participant index, using numpy's `Generator(PCG64(20261115))`: first the
300 calibration participants without replacement, then for each rotation in order two
participants from the rest without replacement, then for each of those one trial drawn
uniformly from their trials on dilemmas not held out.

## Models

| Family | Model | Settings |
|---|---|---|
| Decision model, which decides | `jev-1.14.0` | One question per call, with declared answers |
| Second family, reported | `gemini-2.5-flash-lite` | Temperature 0; thinking off; one output token; log-probabilities of the 20 likeliest tokens |

The version each response reports is recorded. If either model is withdrawn or reports
another version before the run ends, the run stops and gives no verdict.

## Spending

Live calls stop at 150 US dollars. If the cap is reached before the run ends, the run stops
and gives no verdict.

The number of examples is fixed at two before registration, by a bound that needs no
held-out data. `uv run python -m zoon.pilot.report budget` counts the tokens of every judge
request and every calibration request exactly. For the held-out requests it uses an upper
bound: 680 participants, 13 trials, two rotations, each with the longest record any ordering
and any answers could give. It prints the total at the prices in force. Registration goes
ahead only if that total is at most 120 US dollars. If it is not, this file is revised and
reviewed again before registration; examples are never reduced after it.

## Order of work

1. This file, the files above and the analysis code are merged. The repository becomes
   public.
2. Each family's evaluative judge rates the 25 outside rewrites. The stop rule is applied.
3. Each family's judges answer for the 13 task rewrites and the strict questions for all 38.
4. The `prompted` arm is called for the calibration subsample.
5. The held-out participants are loaded. The `prompted` arm is called for them.
6. One command produces the report, which is committed with a digest of every transcript.

Held-out participants are not loaded, counted beyond their number, or rendered into a
request before step 5.

**Repairs.** After step 2 begins, the code may change only to fix a fault that stops it
running, and only if the fix leaves every request, every inclusion rule and every numerical
definition in this file unchanged. Any other change ends the confirmatory run: what follows
is reported as exploratory, and the hypotheses are not decided by it.

## Reported beside the verdict

- H1-strict, and both hypotheses with the second family.
- The reference rows of `lean.md` with no model: `sign`, `coded`, `norm` and `known share`,
  each with learning as well. `zoon-evaluative` minus `sign` with learning is given, since it
  isolates what the judge's rating adds beyond knowing which option the action is.
- Each comparison by experiment, and on trials 2 to 13 only.
- The interval from resampling dilemmas.
- The size of the reading as a share of what `norm` and `known share` achieve.
- The recall checks of `arms.md`: recognition, reversal, and agreement with `norm`. Any
  rewrite the judge's model can name is listed, and every comparison is repeated with those
  dilemmas left out of all arms, `prompted` included.
- Each arm's number of fitted parameters.
- Calls, tokens and cost by family.

## What is not tested

Memory that decays, needs, and decision timing. The spread of behaviour across zoons. Any
dilemma other than these 13.

## Deviations

None.
